\title{Thricing: A Sixth-Order Kernel Estimator of the Risk-Neutral Density from a Single Option Chain}

\author[ifz]{Simon A.\ Broda\corref{cor1}}
\ead{simon.broda@hslu.ch}
\affiliation[ifz]{organization={Institute of Financial Services IFZ, Lucerne University of Applied Sciences},
            addressline={Suurstoffi 1},
            city={Rotkreuz},
            postcode={6343},
            country={Switzerland}}
\cortext[cor1]{Corresponding author.}

\begin{abstract}
The risk-neutral density of an index at a fixed expiry is the second strike derivative of the call price curve, but a listed chain supplies that curve only at a finite, noisy set of strikes. This paper proposes an estimator that works on the normalized call, which is the survival function of the integrated-tail distribution of the underlying. That function is fitted by a cubic smoothing spline and smoothed by a Gaussian kernel; a three-bandwidth generalized jackknife, which we call thricing, raises the order of the kernel to six and makes the Gaussian convolution of the spline, and hence the density and the fitted call, available in closed form. The bandwidth follows the \(n^{-1/9}\) rate of a second-derivative estimator, and the spline penalty is a closed-form function of the roughness of the interpolant. We prove in-fill consistency under additive noise in the quotes. In a Monte Carlo study with Heston, variance-gamma, and Bates targets, three noise levels, and three strike meshes, the estimator has the smallest or statistically indistinguishable from the smallest integrated squared error in most designs once every competing method is tuned by one common criterion. On listed S\&P~500, Nasdaq-100, and Russell~2000 chains it has the smallest out-of-sample pricing error on every chain, measured in index points, in units of the quoted half-spread, and in implied volatility, while delivering a unimodal density with unit mass. A sensitivity analysis shows that the conclusions do not depend on the bandwidth constant.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\citet{breeden1978} showed that the risk-neutral density of the underlying at a fixed expiry equals the second strike derivative of the European call price curve, scaled by the discount factor. The identity requires a twice differentiable price curve, whereas a market supplies a finite ordered list of strikes and a noisy quote at each. Numerical second differentiation of such a list is ill-posed: interpolants oscillate, their second derivatives change sign, and the result depends on the interpolation scheme. The literature responds with parametric mixtures and Gram--Charlier expansions, implied-volatility interpolants, local polynomials of the call, and the positive convolution approximation of \citet{bondarenko2003}; Section~\ref{sec:lit} reviews these approaches.

This paper proposes a nonparametric estimator that uses the quotes of one maturity and nothing else. Its construction rests on an elementary observation. The call normalized by the dividend-adjusted spot, \(\Ct(K)=C(K)/(S_0e^{-qT})\), decreases from \(1\) at \(K=0\) to \(0\) at \(K=\infty\): it is the survival function of a probability distribution on \((0,\infty)\), namely the integrated-tail (equilibrium) distribution of \(S_T\) under the risk-neutral measure, whose density is the discounted tail probability \((1-F_{\Q}(K))/F\). Its characteristic function is \((\varphi_{\Q}(s)-1)/(\mathrm{i}sF)\), so a model that supplies \(\varphi_{\Q}\) prices calls by one Fourier inversion \citep{lewis2000}, and conversely the quotes determine the characteristic function of \(S_T\). The estimator inverts this identity: it fits the integrated-tail distribution function \(P=1-\Ct\) by a cubic smoothing spline, completes the unquoted wings, and smooths the derivative of the spline by a Gaussian kernel. Differentiating the smoothed curve once more gives the density, and integrating it once gives the fitted call, so the density and the call are two readings of the same object. Both are available in closed form because every piece of the spline is a polynomial.

A Gaussian kernel is of order two and leaves a bias of order \(h^2\). Twicing \citep{tukey1977,schucanysommers1977} removes that term by combining two bandwidths; we combine three, which removes the \(h^4\) term as well and gives an order-six kernel. We call this construction thricing. It is a generalized jackknife in the sense of \citet{schucanysommers1977} and \citet{jonesfoster1993}, and we do not claim it is new in principle; its merit here is that it leaves the estimator in closed form, since the thriced kernel is a signed mixture of three Gaussians. Because \(P\) is two derivatives smoother than the density, the leading bias of a Gaussian smooth of \(P\) is large relative to its variance at the bandwidths that a second derivative calls for, and the higher-order correction pays off in practice: on exact Heston quotes it reduces the integrated squared error by three orders of magnitude on a dense chain.

Two tuning choices remain, and both are rule based. The bandwidth is a bounded multiple of \(n^{-1/9}\), the rate that balances bias and variance of a second-derivative estimator, scaled by the forward times the at-the-money implied volatility times the root of maturity. The spline penalty is zero when the thriced interpolant is as smooth as the thriced kernel itself, measured by a positive-part total-variation score, and otherwise a closed-form function of the excess. Section~\ref{sec:rules} states both rules and explains every constant; Section~\ref{sec:sens} shows that the results are insensitive to the bandwidth constant across the range \(0.20\) to \(0.60\), and that the penalty matters only on noisy chains. Theorem~\ref{thm:cons} gives in-fill consistency under additive noise in the quotes, with a data-dependent penalty, whenever the strike mesh is quasi-uniform, \(h\to 0\) and \(nh^5/(\log n)^2\to\infty\).

The empirical contribution is a comparison under a protocol in which no method is handicapped by its tuning. Every estimator that has a bandwidth---the positive convolution approximation of \citeauthor{bondarenko2003}, the implied-volatility kernel of \citet{aitsahalia1998}, the shape-constrained local linear of \citet{aitsahalia2003}, the local cubics of \citet{grith2012}, and our own---is tuned by the same criterion, even/odd out-of-sample pricing error on one geometric bandwidth grid, and the tuning is nested inside the hold-out. The rules that the source papers propose are reported next to the tuned versions. Pricing errors are measured in index points, in units of the quoted half-spread, and in implied volatility, so that chains of different levels can be averaged and so that an error can be judged against the market's own uncertainty. Three targets (Heston, variance gamma, and the Bates jump diffusion), three noise levels, and three strike meshes enter the Monte Carlo study, with \mcrepsmain{} replications and standard errors. On the listed side, the four chains of the main table are complemented by every remaining standard monthly expiry in the same snapshots, \nextra{} chains in all, scored with the rules held fixed.

The results are as follows. On exact quotes, tails and thricing reduce the error of the plain spline by several orders of magnitude, and the rule bandwidth is close to the oracle on sparse chains. With noise, the estimator has the smallest mean integrated squared error, or one within two standard errors of the smallest, in most designs; the cross-validated implied-volatility smoother of \citeauthor{aitsahalia1998} is its closest competitor and is occasionally better on the dense mesh. On the listed chains the estimator has the smallest nested hold-out error on each of the four main chains under all three metrics, while keeping one mode, unit mass, and no negative mass to three decimals; its advantage over the next method is between a factor of about two and ten in half-spread units. Every good method prices inside the quoted spread, so the economic content of the listed comparison is in the density diagnostics as much as in the pricing numbers. We also find that pricing cross-validation under-smooths on dense noisy chains for every method, including ours, which is an argument for a rate-based rule.

The paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:lit} reviews related work. Section~\ref{sec:est} constructs the estimator and states the tuning rules. Section~\ref{sec:ifc} gives the asymptotic result. Section~\ref{sec:sim} reports the simulation study and the sensitivity analysis. Section~\ref{sec:emp} describes the data, the comparison protocol, and the listed results. Section~\ref{sec:conc} concludes. The proof, the computational details, and the full tables are in the appendices.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:lit}

The identity of \citet{breeden1978} is the continuous-strike version of a spanning argument of \citet{ross1976}: with a put at every strike, the second derivative of the put curve is a density and any sufficiently regular payoff can be synthesized. \citet{dupire1994} obtained the analogous result in calendar time. Observed markets supply neither a continuum of strikes nor noiseless quotes.

A first family of methods specifies the density of \(S_T\) parametrically and fits the implied prices to the quotes. \citet{ritchey1990} and \citet{melick1997} use mixtures of lognormals, \citet{bahra1997} and \citet{soderlind1997} related specifications for interest-rate and foreign-exchange options. Two or three components suffice for a unimodal skewed density, but the fit is poorly identified once more components are admitted, and the approximation to a stochastic-volatility or jump-diffusion density need not improve as more quotes arrive. Expansion methods perturb a baseline density by Gram--Charlier or Hermite terms \citep{jarrowrudd1982,corradosu1996,rompolis2007,xiu2014}; they match a finite number of moments but are not guaranteed to stay nonnegative.

A second family, the most common in applied work, interpolates the Black--Scholes implied-volatility function and differentiates the implied call twice. \citet{shimko1993} fitted a quadratic in strike, \citet{malz1997} a cubic spline in delta, \citet{bliss2002} a smoothing spline, and \citet{figlewski2010} completes the tails with a generalized extreme-value distribution. Implied volatility is a flat function of strike, so a low-order interpolant has small bias when quotes are accurate; the drawback is that the transformation inflates price noise where vega is small, and an interpolating spline then treats noisy tail quotes as exact. \citet{jackwerth2004} and \citet{figlewski2018} note that a cubic spline of implied volatility puts kinks in the second derivative and recommend higher-degree splines. Shape-constrained smoothers of the surface \citep{fengler2005,fengler2009,gatheraljacquier2014,ludwig2015} and neural smoothers \citep{ackerer2020} address the sign problem at the cost of a more involved optimization.

A third family estimates the call, or its implied volatility, by kernel regression or local polynomials and differentiates. \citet{aitsahalia1998} use a Nadaraya--Watson kernel on \(14{,}431\) S\&P~500 options pooled across 1993, because a single expiry was treated as too short a list; \citet{aitsahalia2000} recovered an implied risk-aversion function from the same estimator. \citet{aitsahalia2003} work one day at a time on twenty to fifty strikes and impose monotonicity and convexity of the call; they report that unconstrained local polynomial estimates of the density took negative values on nearly every day of 1999 and that the raw quotes violated convexity on \(24\%\) of the prints. \citet{grith2012} survey this literature and estimate a local cubic of the rescaled call with a quartic kernel; they find the cross-validated bandwidth too wiggly and plot their densities at twice that width. \citet{songxiu2016} use a local linear smoother of the implied volatility of a single maturity on a large panel. Kernel estimates of regression derivatives go back to \citet{gassermuller1984}; the optimal bandwidth for a second derivative is of order \(n^{-1/9}\) \citep{fangijbels1996}. \citet{fanmancini2009} combine a parametric guide with a nonparametric correction, and \citet{yatchew2006}, \citet{birke2009} and \citet{monteiro2008} study shape-constrained least squares.

\citet{bondarenko2003} proposed the positive convolution approximation (PCA): the density is the convolution of a positive kernel with a discrete probability chosen to minimize the squared pricing error. The result is a density by construction, and in his comparison it dominated mixtures, expansions and several regularization methods; \citet{lu2019} report the same ranking on affine jump-diffusions. \citet{rubinstein1994}, \citet{jackwerth1996}, \citet{stutzer1996} and \citet{buchen1996} recover probabilities on a grid by minimizing a distance to a prior under pricing constraints; \citet{cont2004} and \citet{monnier2014} treat the problem as a linear inverse problem.

The estimator of this paper is a kernel smooth of the derivative of a spline fitted to the integrated-tail distribution function of one maturity, with a sixth-order Gaussian jackknife kernel. Comparisons of methods on market data use pricing error or stability as the criterion \citep{cooper1999,jondeau2000,coutant2001,anagnou2005,bliss2002,liu2007}; comparisons on a known process are rarer \citep{bondarenko2003,lai2014,santos2015,lu2019}. Section~\ref{sec:sim} is of the second kind and Section~\ref{sec:emp} of the first. Relative to those studies, the novelty of our comparison is the tuning protocol: every method is tuned by the same out-of-sample criterion, the source papers' own rules are shown alongside, and the errors are expressed in half-spreads and implied volatility.

\section{The Estimator}
\label{sec:est}

\subsection{The call as the survival function of the integrated-tail distribution}
\label{sec:construct}

Assume a risk-neutral measure \(\Q\) under which \(S_T\) has a density \(f_{\Q}\) on \((0,\infty)\) with \(\E^{\Q}[S_T]<\infty\). Let \(r\) be the continuously compounded rate, \(q\) the dividend yield, and \(F=S_0e^{(r-q)T}=\E^{\Q}[S_T]\) the forward. The call curve is \(C(K)=e^{-rT}\int_K^{\infty}(x-K)f_{\Q}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\), and we define the normalized call
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Ctil}
\Ct(K)
\;\equiv\;
\frac{C(K)}{S_0e^{-qT}}
\;=\;
\frac1F\int_K^{\infty}(x-K)\,f_{\Q}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x .
\end{equation}
\(\Ct\) is convex, decreasing, equal to \(1\) at \(K=0\) and to \(0\) at infinity, so \(P\equiv 1-\Ct\) is a distribution function on \([0,\infty)\) with density
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Pdens}
P'(K)\;=\;\frac{1-F_{\Q}(K)}{F},
\qquad
P''(K)\;=\;-\frac{f_{\Q}(K)}{F}.
\end{equation}
\(P\) is the integrated-tail, or equilibrium, distribution of \(S_T\). Writing \(\varphi_{\Q}(s)=\E^{\Q}[e^{\mathrm{i}sS_T}]\) and \(\varphi_P(s)=\int e^{\mathrm{i}sK}\,\mathrm{d}P(K)\), an integration by parts gives, for \(s\neq 0\),
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:phi-P}
\varphi_P(s)\;=\;\frac{\varphi_{\Q}(s)-1}{\mathrm{i}s\,F},
\qquad
\varphi_{\Q}(s)\;=\;1+\mathrm{i}s\,F\,\varphi_P(s).
\end{equation}
Read from left to right, \eqref{eq:phi-P} is a pricing formula \citep{lewis2000}. This paper reads it from right to left: \(\varphi_P\), and hence \(P\), is estimated from the quotes, and the density follows from \eqref{eq:Pdens} as \(f_{\Q}=-FP''\). The Fourier form is useful because smoothing by a Gaussian kernel and differentiation are both multipliers of \(\varphi_P\), and because the pricing direction supplies the exact quotes of Section~\ref{sec:sim}.

\subsection{Smoothing spline and Gaussian smoothing}
\label{sec:spline}

Observed calls at strikes \(0<K_1<\cdots<K_m\) supply \(P_i=1-C_i/(S_0e^{-qT})\). The estimator fits the natural cubic smoothing spline \(\Pcs\) that minimizes
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:smooth-penalty}
\sum_i\bigl(P_i-g(K_i)\bigr)^2
\;+\;
c\,F^3\int\bigl(g''(K)\bigr)^2\,\mathrm{d}K ,
\end{equation}
over the knots described below. The factor \(F^3\) makes \(c\) dimensionless. At \(c=0\) the spline interpolates. Let \(\kappa_h\) be the Gaussian density with standard deviation \(h\) and \(\mathcal K_h\) its distribution function. The density and the fitted distribution function are
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cubic}
\fhat(K)
\;=\;
-F\,\bigl(\Pcs{}'\ast\kappa_h\bigr)'(K),
\qquad
\hat P(K)
\;=\;
\bigl(\Pcs{}'\ast\mathcal K_h\bigr)(K),
\end{equation}
and the fitted call is \(\hat C=S_0e^{-qT}(1-\hat P)\). In the Fourier domain \eqref{eq:cubic} multiplies \(\varphi_{\Pcs}\) by \(e^{-h^2s^2/2}\) and by \(\mathrm{i}s\). In the strike domain, each knot interval of \(\Pcs{}'\) is a quadratic, so each term of \eqref{eq:cubic} is an integral of a quadratic against a Gaussian and is elementary; \ref{app:compute} describes three ways of evaluating the sum, the fastest of which takes a few milliseconds on a chain of four hundred strikes.

At \(c=0\), \eqref{eq:cubic} is a kernel estimator of the second derivative of \(P\) in the sense of \citet{gassermuller1984}, with the interpolating spline in place of the step function. The spline matters: a piecewise-linear interpolant has a derivative with jumps at the knots, whose convolution with \(\kappa_h'\) oscillates at the scale of the mesh unless \(h\) is many knot spacings wide. The cubic spline has a continuous second derivative, so the convolution is smooth on the scale of \(h\) whenever \(h\) exceeds about one knot spacing, and the bias is governed by the kernel rather than by the mesh.

\subsection{Completing the tails}
\label{sec:tails}

The spline stops at the first and last quotes, and \(P\) is a distribution function on \((0,\infty)\). On \((0,K_1]\) a deep in-the-money call is intrinsic, \(\Ct(K)=1-K/F\), so \(P(K)=K/F\) is a straight line through the origin. The estimator includes the knot \(P(0)=0\) and otherwise lets the spline pass through \(P(K_1)\); when the first quote is at intrinsic the fitted segment is exactly \(K/F\). On the right, if the last quoted call \(C_m\) is positive the residual call is continued linearly, with the slope of the last strictly negative step of the quoted call clipped to \([-e^{-rT},0)\), until it reaches zero at \(K_{\mathrm{end}}\le 8F\), and the knot \(P(K_{\mathrm{end}})=1\) is added. A flat last step, two quotes on the premium floor, does not count as a decay. If \(C_m=0\) the right wing is empty. The tail knots enter \eqref{eq:smooth-penalty} like the quoted ones.

The tails matter for the second-derivative estimate even far from the wings. A natural spline imposes \(P''=0\) at its end knots, which lie inside the strike window when only the quotes are used; the completion moves those boundary conditions to \(0\) and to \(K_{\mathrm{end}}\). The higher-order kernel of the next section has negative sidelobes of width proportional to \(h\), and on a truncated strip those sidelobes amplify the missing mass. Table~\ref{tab:exact} quantifies the effect: on a sparse chain the error of the quoted spline falls by four orders of magnitude when the wings are added.

\subsection{Thricing: a sixth-order Gaussian jackknife kernel}
\label{sec:thrice}

The Gaussian kernel has second moment \(h^2\), so the bias of \eqref{eq:cubic} is of order \(h^2\). The generalized jackknife of \citet{schucanysommers1977} removes the leading term by combining estimates at two bandwidths; \citet{tukey1977} called the simplest such combination twicing. Replacing \(e^{-h^2s^2/2}\) by \(2e^{-h^2s^2/2}-e^{-h^2s^2}\), the transform of \(2\kappa_h-\kappa_{h\sqrt2}\), cancels the second moment and leaves a bias of order \(h^4\). Taking the construction one step further, the combination
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:phi-thrice}
\tfrac83\,e^{-h^2s^2/2}-2\,e^{-h^2s^2}+\tfrac13\,e^{-2h^2s^2}
\end{equation}
is the transform of \(L_h=\tfrac83\kappa_h-2\kappa_{h\sqrt2}+\tfrac13\kappa_{2h}\), whose moments of orders two and four vanish. We call this thricing. \(L_h\) is a sixth-order kernel in the sense of \citet{jonesfoster1993}, and the estimator becomes
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:thrice}
\fhat^{\mathrm{th}}
\;=\;
\tfrac83\,\fhat{}_{,h}-2\,\fhat{}_{,h\sqrt2}+\tfrac13\,\fhat{}_{,2h},
\end{equation}
with the same combination applied to \(\hat P\). The bias is of order \(h^6\) when \(f_{\Q}\) has a bounded sixth derivative. Because \(L_h\) takes negative values, \(\fhat^{\mathrm{th}}\) is not a density by construction; Sections~\ref{sec:sim} and~\ref{sec:emp} report its negative mass, which is zero to three decimals on every listed chain. The fitted \(\hat P^{\mathrm{th}}\) is clipped to \([0,1]\) before pricing and premiums are floored at zero.

The correction is large in this problem for a specific reason. The call is a double integral of the density, so \(P\) is two derivatives smoother than \(f_{\Q}\), and the bias of a kernel smooth of \(P\) at the bandwidth that a second derivative requires, \(h\asymp n^{-1/9}\), dominates the variance of order \((nh^5)^{-1}\). On exact quotes that bias is the entire error; twicing removes its leading term and thricing the next. Jackknifing is not free: \(\int|L_h|=1.196\), so the variance constant rises by about \(43\%\) relative to the Gaussian, and the sixth-order bias constant involves the sixth derivative of \(f_{\Q}\). Table~\ref{tab:exact} and Table~\ref{tab:mc} show that the trade is favourable at the bandwidths the rule selects.

\subsection{The bandwidth and penalty rules}
\label{sec:rules}

Two constants have to be chosen: the bandwidth \(h\) in \eqref{eq:thrice} and the penalty \(c\) in \eqref{eq:smooth-penalty}. Both are set by rules, not by cross-validation, because Section~\ref{sec:emp} shows that pricing cross-validation under-smooths a density estimator, and because a rule makes the estimator deterministic given the chain.

\paragraph{Bandwidth}
A kernel estimator of a second derivative balances a squared bias of order \(h^{4}\) (order two kernel) against a variance of order \((nh^5)^{-1}\), which gives \(h\asymp n^{-1/9}\) \citep{fangijbels1996}; the familiar density rate \(n^{-1/5}\) leaves \(nh^5\) bounded and is not usable. The natural scale of a chain is \(F\hat\sigma_{\mathrm{ATM}}\sqrt T\), the standard deviation of \(S_T\) under a lognormal at the at-the-money implied volatility \(\hat\sigma_{\mathrm{ATM}}\), taken as the median implied volatility of contracts with \(|K-F|\le 0.03F\). The rule is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:h-d2}
h
\;=\;
c_0\,\gamma\,F\,\hat\sigma_{\mathrm{ATM}}\sqrt T\,n^{-1/9},
\qquad
c_0=0.34,
\end{equation}
with \(n\) the number of quoted strikes, and \(\gamma\in[0.90,1.10]\) a correction for the shape of the distribution: the interquartile range of the positive second differences of the quoted call (the butterfly masses, a crude histogram of \(f_{\Q}\)) divided by the normal interquartile range \(2\Phi^{-1}(3/4)F\hat\sigma_{\mathrm{ATM}}\sqrt T\), clipped to that interval. The factor \(\gamma\) widens the bandwidth on fat-tailed chains and narrows it on peaked ones; on the listed chains of Section~\ref{sec:emp} it is at its lower bound. The constant \(c_0=0.34\) was chosen by inspection on the exact Heston and variance-gamma designs and on the four listed chains of Table~\ref{tab:listed}. Its derivation is not from an asymptotic formula, and Section~\ref{sec:sens} therefore reports the estimator at \(c_0\in\{0.20,0.27,0.34,0.45,0.60\}\): the hold-out error on the listed chains varies by less than one part in ten over that range and the Monte Carlo ranking does not change. Any bounded multiple of \(n^{-1/9}\) satisfies the condition of Theorem~\ref{thm:cons}.

\paragraph{Penalty}
On exact quotes the interpolating spline is the right choice and the penalty should be zero; on noisy quotes an interpolant transmits the noise to the second derivative. The rule reads the roughness of the thriced interpolant, the estimator at \(c=0\), from a score that is one for a unimodal curve and larger otherwise. For a nonnegative function \(g\) on a window \([a,b]\), let
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:tv}
\TV(g)\;=\;\frac{g(a)+\int_a^b|g'|+g(b)}{2\max g},
\end{equation}
the total variation of \(g\) completed to zero at the two endpoints, divided by twice its maximum. A unimodal curve scores exactly one; an additional mode raises the score by its height relative to the maximum. The score is applied to the positive part of the thriced interpolant on \([0.60F,1.30F]\), a window that holds the bulk of the mass on every chain we have seen and avoids the far wings, where the estimate is dominated by the tail completion. The thriced kernel itself, scored the same way, gives \(\TV_*=1.007\), slightly above one because of its sidelobes, and a nonnegative kernel of the same height has signed variation \(v_*=\int|L_1|=1.196\), so one excess unit of variation \(v_*-1=0.196\) is what one extra sidelobe of the kernel contributes. The rule counts the excess of the interpolant over the allowance in those units, \(\rho=(\TV-\TV_*)_+/(v_*-1)\), and chooses \(c\) so that the smoothing spline attenuates the frequency \(1/h\) by the factor \(1/(1+\rho)\). On a uniform mesh of spacing \(\delta\) the smoothing spline multiplies a Fourier mode of frequency \(\omega\) by \(1/(1+cF^3\delta\omega^4)\) \citep{green1994}, which gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:penalty-rule}
c
\;=\;
\frac{(\TV-\TV_*)_+}{v_*-1}\;\frac{h^4}{\delta\,F^3},
\end{equation}
with \(\delta\) the median knot spacing. The interpolant is computed once, the score once, and the penalized spline once. The rule does not count modes: a bimodal target raises \(c\) through the score, but a second mode that survives the smoothing is kept, as it is on two of the long-dated chains in \ref{app:extra}. On exact quotes of every design in Section~\ref{sec:sim} the score is below the allowance and the penalty is zero. The constants \(0.60\), \(1.30\), and the choice of the kernel's own score as the allowance are conventions; Section~\ref{sec:sens} shows the estimator with the penalty switched off.

\section{Asymptotics}
\label{sec:ifc}

Write \(P\) for the integrated-tail distribution function of Section~\ref{sec:construct}, so that \(f_{\Q}=-FP''\) on \((0,\infty)\), and let the estimator be the thriced smoothing spline \eqref{eq:thrice} with the tails of Section~\ref{sec:tails}, the bandwidth \eqref{eq:h-d2}, and the penalty \eqref{eq:penalty-rule}.

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:cons}
Let \(\E^{\Q}[S_T]<\infty\), let \(f_{\Q}\) be four times continuously differentiable on \((0,\infty)\), and let \(C\) be the corresponding call curve. Fix a compact interval \([A,B]\subset(0,\infty)\). Consider a sequence of strike grids \(0<K_1^{(m)}<\cdots<K_m^{(m)}\) with maximum mesh \(\bar\delta_m\to 0\), \(K_1^{(m)}\le A\), \(K_m^{(m)}\ge B\), and quasi-uniform on \([A,B]\): \(n_m\bar\delta_m\) is bounded above and below, where \(n_m\) is the number of strikes in \([A,B]\). Observe
\[
P_i^{(m)}\;=\;P\bigl(K_i^{(m)}\bigr)+\eta_i^{(m)},
\]
with \(\eta_i^{(m)}\) independent, \(\E[\eta_i^{(m)}]=0\), and \(\E[(\eta_i^{(m)})^2]\le\tau^2<\infty\). Let the window \([0.60F,1.30F]\) of \eqref{eq:penalty-rule} lie inside \((A,B)\) and let \(f_{\Q}\) be positive somewhere on it; let \(F\) be fixed. Let \(h_m\to 0\) and \(n_mh_m^5/(\log n_m)^2\to\infty\). Then \(\fhat^{\mathrm{th}}\to f_{\Q}\) in probability, uniformly on every compact subset of \((A,B)\).
\end{theorem}

The proof is in \ref{app:proof}. Its structure is as follows. At penalty zero, the estimator is a kernel estimator of \(P''\) applied to the interpolating spline, and its noise part is within \(o_p(1)\) of a kernel sum with mean zero and variance of order \(\tau^2/(n_mh_m^5)\), uniformly on compacts; the tail knots contribute a term of order \(h^{-2}\exp(-\varepsilon^2/2h^2)\) on any compact that stays \(\varepsilon\) away from the quoted range. The data-dependent penalty is handled by bounding the random smoothing-spline half-width \(\beta=(cF^3\delta)^{1/4}\) by a deterministic sequence \(b_m\to 0\) with probability tending to one, and by a union bound over a geometric grid of \(O((\log n_m)^2)\) widths; the \((\log n_m)^2\) in the bandwidth condition pays for that grid. Since \eqref{eq:h-d2} is a bounded multiple of \(n^{-1/9}\) and \(n^{4/9}/(\log n)^2\to\infty\), the rule bandwidth satisfies the condition.

\begin{remark}
\label{rem:rate}
At penalty zero and under six continuous derivatives of \(f_{\Q}\), the bias of \(\fhat^{\mathrm{th}}\) is of order \(h^6+\bar\delta^4\), the second term from cubic spline interpolation of a \(C^4\) function, and the standard deviation of order \((nh^5)^{-1/2}\); the pointwise rate at \(h\asymp n^{-1/17}\) would then be \(n^{-6/17}\), the rate of a sixth-order kernel estimator of a second derivative. The rule \eqref{eq:h-d2} uses the second-order exponent \(n^{-1/9}\), which is conservative for the variance; the simulation evidence in Section~\ref{sec:sim} shows that on chains of \(32\) to \(256\) strikes the rule bandwidth is within a factor of two of the oracle. A formal rate and a limiting distribution for the penalized estimator are left for future work.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
The empirical section fits the spline to the decreasing-convex projection of the quoted calls rather than to the raw quotes. When the true call lies in the interior of the cone of decreasing convex functions, the projection changes the fitted values by an amount of the same stochastic order as \(\eta_i\), and the theorem applies unchanged. The theorem does not require the estimator to be a density, and \(\fhat^{\mathrm{th}}\) is not one in finite samples; the negative mass vanishes in the limit with the sidelobes.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Designs}
\label{sec:designs}

Three data-generating processes are used. The first is the stochastic-volatility model of \citet{heston1993} with the equity parameters of \citet{bakshi1997}: \(S_0=100\), \(r=0.05\), \(q=0\), \(T=1/4\), mean reversion \(\kappa_v=1.15\), long-run variance \(\theta_v=0.04\), volatility of variance \(\sigma_v=0.39\), correlation \(\rho_v=-0.64\), and \(v_0=0.04\). The second is the variance-gamma process of \citet{madan1998} with the parameters of \citet{carrmadan1999}: \(\sigma=0.12\), \(\nu=0.2\), \(\theta=-0.14\), a pure-jump L\'evy process whose density of \(S_T\) is more peaked than Heston's. The third is the jump diffusion of \citet{bates1996} with the SVJ estimates of \citet{bakshi1997}: \(\kappa_v=2.03\), \(\theta_v=0.04\), \(\sigma_v=0.38\), \(\rho_v=-0.52\), jump intensity \(\lambda=0.59\), mean log jump \(\mu_J=-0.05\), and jump volatility \(\sigma_J=0.07\). Call prices are computed from the characteristic function by the damped integrand of \citet{carrmadan1999} on \(4096\) frequencies in \((0,250]\), and the true density of \(S_T\) is the Fourier inversion of the same characteristic function on the same grid; for variance gamma the density is the Bessel-function formula of \citet{madan1998} and the calls are discounted payoffs under it.

Three strike meshes are used. The dense chain has \(m=256\) equally spaced strikes on \([30,220]\) (spacing \(0.745\)), the sparse chain \(m=32\) equally spaced strikes on \([70,140]\) (spacing \(2.26\)), and the listed chain is the S\&P~500 December mesh of Section~\ref{sec:emp} rescaled to a forward of \(100\) and thinned to \(110\) strikes, so that it is fine near the money and coarse in the wings. Integrated squared error (ISE) is \(\int_{60}^{150}(\fhat-f_{\Q})^2\,\mathrm{d}K\), evaluated by the trapezoidal rule on \(401\) points, and is reported multiplied by \(10^4\) in the Monte Carlo tables.

\paragraph{Results}
At \(\mathrm{sd}=0.01\) the rule estimator has the smallest mean ISE, or one within two standard errors of the smallest, in seven of the nine designs of Table~\ref{tab:mc}: all three meshes on Heston and on Bates, and the listed mesh on variance gamma. Its advantage is largest on the listed mesh, which is the most realistic of the three, where the next method is PCA at four to five times the error on Heston and Bates. On the dense mesh the margin over PCA is a factor of \(1.8\) on Heston and \(1.9\) on Bates. The two exceptions are both variance gamma, whose density is much more peaked than the other two: on the dense mesh PCA is better by a factor of two, and on the sparse mesh the cross-validated implied-volatility kernel of \citeauthor{aitsahalia1998} is better by a factor of \(2.6\). Table~\ref{tab:sens} shows that the rule bandwidth is wider than the variance-gamma optimum, but even the best constant does not close the gap on the dense chain, so the peak is where the sixth-order kernel's negative sidelobes cost most.

The cross-validated kernel smoothers, the implied-volatility kernel, the local linear and the two local cubics, are one to two orders of magnitude worse than the rule on the dense and listed meshes. Their price cross-validation selects the grid floor, as on the listed chains, and the resulting second derivative is noise; on the sparse mesh, where the grid floor is wider relative to the strike range, the same methods are competitive, and the implied-volatility kernel is within a factor of \(1.3\) of the rule on Heston. The source papers' own rules do better than their cross-validated versions on the dense mesh and worse on the sparse one, the plug-in of \citeauthor{aitsahalia2003} being the most accurate of them on dense chains. ``Ours, CV'' is also affected: it is within two standard errors of the rule on the sparse mesh and three to twelve times worse on the listed mesh. The rule bandwidth does not suffer from this, because it is tied to the scale of the distribution and to \(n^{-1/9}\) rather than to a pricing criterion that cannot see the second derivative.

\subsection{Methods and tuning protocol}
\label{sec:methods}

Five estimators from the literature are compared with ours. \emph{PCA} is the positive convolution approximation of \citeauthor{bondarenko2003}: a Gaussian kernel on the spot convolved with a discrete probability on equally spaced nodes from the first quoted strike to the last, with the node spacing equal to the bandwidth and the count between \(12\) and \(61\); the weights are nonnegative, sum to one, have mean \(F\), and minimize the squared pricing error, each node pricing a call by the Bachelier formula. \emph{AS--Lo} is the estimator of \citeauthor{aitsahalia1998} on one maturity, where the semiparametric regression reduces to Nadaraya--Watson of Black--Scholes implied volatility on moneyness \(K/F\) with a Gaussian kernel; prices are Black--Scholes at the smoothed volatility and the density is the strike derivative of that call. \emph{AS--Duarte} is the shape-constrained estimator of \citeauthor{aitsahalia2003}: the projection above followed by a Gaussian local linear regression; the price is the local level and the density is \(e^{rT}\) times the strike derivative of the local slope, scaled to unit mass and shifted to mean \(F\) as in their Section 3.6. \emph{GHS call} and \emph{GHS IV} are the local cubics of \citeauthor{grith2012} with the quartic kernel, of the call divided by the spot and of implied volatility against strike; the density is \(e^{rT}\) times the second derivative of the local cubic for the call, and the implied-volatility curvature formula for the smile.

Each of these has one bandwidth. The common protocol tunes it by even/odd cross-validation of the out-of-the-money pricing error: the strikes are split into even and odd positions, the method is fitted on one half and scored on the other, the two directions are averaged, and the bandwidth minimizing that average on a geometric grid of \(19\) points from \(0.04\) to \(3\) times \(F\hat\sigma_{\mathrm{ATM}}\sqrt T\), floored at one and a half median strike gaps, is kept. The same grid, in strike units, is used for every method; for AS--Lo it is divided by \(F\). Our estimator is tuned the same way (``Ours, CV''), with the penalty \eqref{eq:penalty-rule} applied at each candidate bandwidth, and is also reported at the rule \eqref{eq:h-d2} (``Ours, rule''). The source papers' own choices are reported as well: the Appendix A bandwidth of \citeauthor{aitsahalia1998}, which on a single maturity is about one standard deviation of moneyness; the \citet{fangijbels1996} plug-in of \citeauthor{aitsahalia2003}, Section 3.5, which uses the level rate \(n^{-1/5}\); and the leave-one-out bandwidth of \citeauthor{grith2012} together with twice that width, which is what their figures use. Those rows are in \ref{app:rules}.

Hold-out is nested. For each of the two even/odd directions, the projection and the tuning are recomputed on the training half alone, including the rule bandwidth \eqref{eq:h-d2} with the training \(n\); the fitted call is then scored on the held-out half against the quoted mids. Errors are reported in three units: index points, the quoted half-spread of the held-out option, and Black--Scholes implied volatility in basis points, each as a root mean square over the held-out strikes. The half-spread unit makes chains of different levels comparable and sets the error against the market's own precision; an error of one means the fitted price sits at the quoted bid or ask. The implied-volatility unit is the one practitioners use, and a fitted premium so far off that no implied volatility exists is counted as one hundred percent. Densities are evaluated on \([\max(50,0.2F),2.4F]\), and we report their positive mass, their negative mass \(\int\fhat^-\), the number of modes on \([0.60F,1.30F]\) (local maxima above a fifth of the global one), and the total-variation score \eqref{eq:tv} on that window.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conc}

The normalized call of a single maturity is the survival function of the integrated-tail distribution of the underlying, and the risk-neutral density is minus the forward times the second derivative of that distribution function. The estimator of this paper fits that function by a cubic smoothing spline, completes the unquoted wings, and smooths the derivative of the spline by a sixth-order Gaussian jackknife kernel; the convolution is in closed form, the density and the fitted call are two readings of one object, and the bandwidth and penalty follow rules that need no search. The estimator is in-fill consistent under additive noise.

The evidence is as follows. On exact quotes the wings and the higher-order kernel reduce the error of a plain spline by several orders of magnitude. On noisy quotes the estimator is the most accurate, or statistically tied with the most accurate, in most of the Heston, variance-gamma and Bates designs, with the implied-volatility kernel of \citeauthor{aitsahalia1998} as the closest competitor. On listed index chains it has the smallest nested out-of-sample pricing error on each chain in index points, half-spreads and implied volatility, and it is the only method among those compared that delivers one mode, unit mass, and no negative mass on every chain; the same ordering holds on average over \nextra{} further chains scored with the rules fixed. The comparison also documents that pricing cross-validation under-smooths every density estimator on dense chains, which argues for rate-based bandwidth rules of the kind used here.

Several extensions are natural. The theorem gives consistency; a rate and a limiting distribution for the penalized estimator, and hence confidence bands, would make the estimator usable for inference about tail probabilities and pricing kernels. The tail completion is linear and could be replaced by a parametric tail fitted to the last quotes, as in \citet{figlewski2010}. Finally, a panel of dates rather than one snapshot would allow the stability of the estimate over time to be assessed, which is the criterion most of the applied literature uses.

\section{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:cons}}
\label{app:proof}

\begin{proof}
Write \(h=h_m\), \(n=n_m\), and \(\delta\) for the median knot spacing, which the two tail gaps do not move. The tail knots lie outside \([A,B]\). On a compact subset of \((A,B)\) at distance at least \(\varepsilon\) from the quoted range, their contribution to \eqref{eq:thrice} is of order \(h^{-2}\exp(-\varepsilon^2/2h^2)\), whether or not the intercept \(K_{\mathrm{end}}\) depends on the last noisy slope, because that intercept lies inside a fixed multiple of \(F\). Quasi-uniformity keeps \(n\delta\) bounded above and below.

\emph{Penalty zero.} The natural cubic interpolant of a \(C^4\) function converges to the function, and its first derivative to the first derivative, uniformly on interior compacts. The thriced kernel \(L_h\) integrates to one and is even, so convolution against it, followed by one derivative, sends \(P'\) to \(P''\) as \(h\to 0\); when \(f_{\Q}\) is six times continuously differentiable the moments of orders two and four vanish and the bias is \(O(h^6)\). The noise part of the estimator is within \(o_p(1)\) of
\[
\sum_i\eta_i\,\delta_i\,h^{-3}\,\Lambda\bigl((K-K_i)/h\bigr),
\]
where \(\Lambda\) is a fixed integrable combination of second derivatives of \(L_1\) with \(\int\Lambda=0\). The sum has mean zero and variance of order \(\tau^2/(nh^5)\), uniformly on the compact. Chebyshev's inequality and a Lipschitz constant of the same order give uniform convergence in probability. The thriced interpolant is therefore consistent for \(f_{\Q}\) on the window of \eqref{eq:penalty-rule}, and its maximum stays bounded away from zero in probability.

\emph{The random penalty.} Write \(Z\) for the error of the interpolant on the window and \(\rho=(\TV-\TV_*)_+/(v_*-1)\). Since \(\TV_*>1\), \(\rho\le(\TV-1)_+/(v_*-1)\). Differentiating the kernel sum multiplies the pointwise variance by \(h^{-2}\); Cauchy--Schwarz then bounds the expected total variation of \(Z\) by a multiple of \((nh^7)^{-1/2}\), and the variation of \(f_{\Q}\) on the window is finite, so \(\rho=O_p(1+(nh^7)^{-1/2})\). The half-width \(\beta=(cF^3\delta)^{1/4}\) of the smoothing-spline multiplier \(1/(1+(\beta\omega)^4)\) satisfies \(\beta^4=O_p(h^4+\sqrt{h/n})\). Since \(h/n=h^6/(nh^5)\to 0\), \(\beta=o_p(1)\).

That multiplier is the transform of an even kernel of unit mass with vanishing moments of orders one to three and fourth moment of order \(\beta^4\); its poles lie off the real axis, so its tails decay exponentially on the scale \(\beta\). Applied to \(P''\), which is \(C^4\), the bias on an interior compact is of order \(\beta^4\) and tends to zero in probability. Thricing composes the result with three Gaussian approximate identities of vanishing width, and the composition tends to \(f_{\Q}\).

\emph{Uniformity over the penalty.} The penalty is a function of the same noise, so the variances are compared on a deterministic grid rather than pathwise. The bound on \(\beta^4\) supplies a deterministic sequence \(b_m\to 0\) with \(\Prob(\beta>b_m)\to 0\), namely \(b_m^4=(h^4+\sqrt{h/n})\log n\). On \(\{\beta\le b_m\}\) the penalty lies in \([0,b_m^4/\delta]\). Up to the width \(\beta_{\min}=h/(\log n)^2\), and at every frequency the thriced symbol has not already damped below \(1/(nh^5)\), the multiplier differs from one by \(o(1)\), and the noise stays within \(o_p(1)\) of the penalty-zero field. Under \(nh^5/(\log n)^2\to\infty\) one has \(h\gg n^{-1/5}(\log n)^{2/5}\), so the ratio \(b_m/\beta_{\min}\) is at most \(n^{1/20}\) times a power of \(\log n\). A geometric grid from \(\beta_{\min}\) to \(b_m\) with adjacent ratio \(1+1/\log n\) therefore has \(G_m=O((\log n)^2)\) nodes, and \(G_m/(nh^5)\to 0\). In the Demmler--Reinsch basis the \(j\)th coefficient is multiplied by \((1+\lambda\nu_j)^{-1}\) with \(\nu_j\ge 0\), so the variance at each node is at most the penalty-zero variance, and a union bound over the grid sends the maximum over the grid to zero in probability. Adjacent widths change the multiplier by \(O(1/\log n)\) at every frequency, so the oscillation between them is \(o_p(1)\). The noise at the random penalty tends to zero in probability on \(\{\beta\le b_m\}\), and the complementary probability tends to zero. Multiplying by \(-F\) gives \(\fhat^{\mathrm{th}}\to f_{\Q}\).
\end{proof}

\section{Computation}
\label{app:compute}

Equations \eqref{eq:cubic} and \eqref{eq:thrice} ask for one Gaussian convolution of the slope of a cubic spline at three bandwidths, read at every strike. Three evaluations are implemented, and on the designs of Sections~\ref{sec:sim} and~\ref{sec:emp} they agree past the printed precision of every table. The first sums the closed-form cell integrals: on each knot interval the slope is a quadratic, the integrals of \(1\), \(u\) and \(u^2\) against \(\phi\) and \(\Phi\) are elementary, and the cost grows with the number of knots times the number of strikes. The second samples the spline, held flat outside the knots, on a grid one sixty-fourth of the smaller of the median knot spacing and \(h\), padded by ten widths of the widest kernel, and takes one real discrete Fourier transform; \eqref{eq:phi-thrice} is then a single multiplier, differentiation is multiplication by \(\mathrm{i}s\), and the level is the inverse transform minus the value at the left knot. The third keeps the transform but replaces the fine samples by the exact moments through degree eight of the spline and of its second derivative over boxes no wider than one bandwidth, which are closed form because both integrands are polynomials; the transform of the piecewise polynomial is the sum of nine moment transforms, the value and the derivative are packed into one complex inverse transform, and the jumps at the ends of the strip are added as the kernel itself.
